Concept Book: Orthonormal Basis Normal Matrix

Payoff

What the Concept Achieves

A matrix $A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ is normal if it commutes with its conjugate transpose: $A^A = AA^$. The Spectral Theorem for Normal Matrices asserts that every normal matrix admits a complete orthonormal basis of eigenvectors — that is, there exists a unitary matrix $U$ (satisfying $U^*U = I$) such that

$$A = U \Lambda U^*,$$

where $\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$ collects the eigenvalues of $A$. This is the natural endpoint of the linear algebra curriculum because it fuses two threads that might otherwise seem independent: the algebraic question of diagonalisability and the geometric question of orthogonality. For a general matrix these threads can diverge; normality is precisely the condition that forces them to converge.

Worked Example

Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. Because $A$ is real and symmetric it is normal. The eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 1$, $\lambda_2 = 3$, with normalised eigenvectors $\mathbf{u}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1)^\top$ and $\mathbf{u}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(-1,1)^\top$. Setting $U = [\mathbf{u}_1 \mid \mathbf{u}_2]$ one verifies $U^\top U = I$ and $A = U \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix} U^\top$, confirming the decomposition.

Key Theorem

Theorem (Spectral Theorem). $A$ is normal if and only if it is unitarily diagonalisable. In that case the eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal, and any repeated eigenspace possesses an orthonormal basis obtainable by the Gram–Schmidt process.

Proof sketch. The forward direction follows from Schur's theorem: every matrix is unitarily triangularisable; if $A$ is additionally normal, the triangular factor must be diagonal. The converse is immediate from $A = U\Lambda U^*$ and the commutativity of diagonal matrices. $\square$

Lab Cell

import sympy as sp

A = sp.Matrix([[2, -1], [-1, 2]])
P, D = A.diagonalize()          # columns of P are eigenvectors
# Verify unitarity after normalisation
U = sp.GramSchmidt(P.columnspace(), orthonormal=True)
U_mat = sp.Matrix(U).T
sp.pprint(sp.simplify(U_mat * D * U_mat.T))   # should recover A

Invitation

The decomposition $A = U\Lambda U^*$ is not an algebraic curiosity — it is the engine behind principal component analysis, the stability analysis of differential equations, and the measurement postulate of quantum mechanics, where every observable is a Hermitian (hence normal) operator and its spectral decomposition encodes all possible measurement outcomes. Choose any one of these domains and you will find that the orthonormal eigenbasis is not merely a computational convenience but the very language in which the theory is written.
