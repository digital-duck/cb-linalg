Diagonalizable Matrix

Definition. An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over a field $\mathbb{F}$ is called diagonalizable if there exists an invertible matrix $P \in M_n(\mathbb{F})$ and a diagonal matrix $D \in M_n(\mathbb{F})$ such that

$$A = P D P^{-1}.$$

Equivalently, $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if $\mathbb{F}^n$ has a basis consisting entirely of eigenvectors of $A$. The columns of $P$ are those eigenvectors, and the diagonal entries of $D$ are the corresponding eigenvalues.

Worked Example. Let

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The characteristic polynomial is $\det(A - \lambda I) = (3 - \lambda)(2 - \lambda)$, giving distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$.

For $\lambda_1 = 3$: solving $(A - 3I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ yields $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 0)^{\top}$.

For $\lambda_2 = 2$: solving $(A - 2I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ yields $\mathbf{v}_2 = (-1, 1)^{\top}$.

Setting $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $D = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, one verifies that $AP = PD$, confirming $A = PDP^{-1}$.

Key Theorem (Sufficient Condition for Diagonalizability). If an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ has $n$ distinct eigenvalues, then $A$ is diagonalizable.

Proof sketch. Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent. With $n$ such eigenvectors in $\mathbb{F}^n$, they form a basis, so $P$ is invertible and $A = PDP^{-1}$ holds. $\square$

Remark. Distinctness is sufficient but not necessary. A matrix with repeated eigenvalues may still be diagonalizable if each eigenvalue's geometric multiplicity (dimension of its eigenspace) equals its algebraic multiplicity.

Lab Cell.

from sympy import Matrix, pprint

A = Matrix([[3, 1], [0, 2]])

P, D = A.diagonalize()   # raises MatrixError if not diagonalizable
pprint(D)                # diagonal eigenvalue matrix
pprint(P)                # eigenvector columns
print(P * D * P**-1 == A)  # True

Running this cell displays $D = \operatorname{diag}(2, 3)$ and the corresponding $P$, and confirms the factorization identity. Try replacing $A$ with a non-diagonalizable matrix such as $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ to observe the raised exception.
