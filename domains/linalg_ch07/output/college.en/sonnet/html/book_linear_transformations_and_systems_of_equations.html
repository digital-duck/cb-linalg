What This Concept Achieves

The study of linear transformations and systems of linear equations is the organizing principle of linear algebra, and it stands as the natural endpoint of any first course in the subject precisely because it unifies everything that preceded it. A linear transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is a map satisfying

$$T(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u}) + T(\mathbf{v}), \qquad T(c\mathbf{u}) = cT(\mathbf{u})$$

for all vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$ and scalars $c$. Every such map is completely described by a matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ via $T(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x}$, and asking when $T(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{b}$ has a solution is precisely asking when the system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is consistent. The two languages—geometric (transformations) and algebraic (systems)—are therefore the same language.

Worked Example

Let $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be the transformation that rotates every vector by $\theta = \pi/4$ radians. Its matrix is

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\tfrac{\pi}{4} & -\sin\tfrac{\pi}{4} \\ \sin\tfrac{\pi}{4} & \cos\tfrac{\pi}{4} \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

To find which input $\mathbf{x}$ maps to $\mathbf{b} = (1, 0)^\top$, we solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$. Because $\det(A) = 1 \neq 0$, the transformation is invertible and the unique solution is $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, -1)^\top$. The invertibility of $T$ is equivalent to the unique solvability of every associated linear system—a theorem, not a coincidence.

Key Theorem

The Rank–Nullity Theorem. For any linear transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ with matrix $A$,

$$\operatorname{rank}(A) + \operatorname{nullity}(A) = n.$$

This identity governs the solution structure of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$: the nullity measures the dimension of the solution family (degrees of freedom), while the rank measures the dimension of the image (which right-hand sides $\mathbf{b}$ are reachable).

Lab Cell

from sympy import Matrix, pi, cos, sin, Rational, sqrt

A = Matrix([[cos(pi/4), -sin(pi/4)],
            [sin(pi/4),  cos(pi/4)]])
b = Matrix([1, 0])

x = A.solve(b)
print("Solution x =", x)
print("Rank:", A.rank())
print("Nullity:", A.cols - A.rank())

Invitation

Every application of mathematics to the physical and computational world—network flow, image compression, machine learning, quantum mechanics—is ultimately an exercise in understanding a linear transformation and solving the system it defines. Choose one domain that matters to you and ask: what is the transformation, what is the right-hand side, and what does the solution mean? That question, posed precisely and answered rigorously, is the practice of applied mathematics.
