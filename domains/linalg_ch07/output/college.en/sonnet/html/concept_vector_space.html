Vector Space

Definition. A vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$ (typically $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$) is a non-empty set $V$ equipped with two operations — vector addition $+: V \times V \to V$ and scalar multiplication $\cdot: \mathbb{F} \times V \to V$ — satisfying the following eight axioms for all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in V$ and all $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{F}$:

  • Commutativity: $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$
  • Associativity of addition: $(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$
  • Additive identity: there exists $\mathbf{0} \in V$ such that $\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v}$
  • Additive inverse: there exists $-\mathbf{v} \in V$ such that $\mathbf{v} + (-\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}$
  • Compatibility: $\alpha(\beta \mathbf{v}) = (\alpha\beta)\mathbf{v}$
  • Scalar identity: $1 \cdot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$
  • Distributivity over vectors: $\alpha(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = \alpha\mathbf{u} + \alpha\mathbf{v}$
  • Distributivity over scalars: $(\alpha + \beta)\mathbf{v} = \alpha\mathbf{v} + \beta\mathbf{v}$

    • Worked example. Consider the set $\mathbb{R}^2 = \{(x, y) \mid x, y \in \mathbb{R}\}$ with componentwise addition $(x_1, y_1) + (x_2, y_2) = (x_1 + x_2,\, y_1 + y_2)$ and scalar multiplication $\alpha(x, y) = (\alpha x, \alpha y)$. The zero vector is $\mathbf{0} = (0, 0)$, and the additive inverse of $(x, y)$ is $(-x, -y)$. One verifies all eight axioms directly from properties of real arithmetic, confirming that $\mathbb{R}^2$ is a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$. Geometrically, vectors are arrows in the plane; addition corresponds to the parallelogram rule, and scalar multiplication stretches or reflects those arrows.

    Key theorem (Uniqueness of zero and inverses). In any vector space $V$, the additive identity $\mathbf{0}$ is unique, and for each $\mathbf{v} \in V$ the additive inverse $-\mathbf{v}$ is unique. Proof sketch: Suppose $\mathbf{0}'$ is another identity. Then $\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{0}' = \mathbf{0}'$. Uniqueness of inverses follows analogously by assuming two inverses $-\mathbf{v}$ and $-\mathbf{v}'$ and adding $\mathbf{v}$ to both sides of $(-\mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$.

    Lab cell.

    from sympy import symbols, Matrix

x1, y1, x2, y2, alpha = symbols('x1 y1 x2 y2 alpha')
v1 = Matrix([x1, y1])
v2 = Matrix([x2, y2])

print("v1 + v2 =", v1 + v2)           # componentwise addition
print("alpha * v1 =", alpha * v1)      # scalar multiplication
print("zero vector:", v1 - v1)         # additive inverse demonstration
