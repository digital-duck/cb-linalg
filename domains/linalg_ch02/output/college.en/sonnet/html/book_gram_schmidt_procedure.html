Concept Book: Gram Schmidt Procedure

Linalg Ch02 · Generated by SPL

Payoff

The Gram-Schmidt procedure is the constructive heart of linear algebra: given any linearly independent set of vectors, it produces an orthonormal basis for their span. Where earlier sections of this book established what orthogonality means and why it matters, Gram-Schmidt answers the operational question—how do we manufacture it on demand? That shift from existence to construction is what makes the procedure a natural capstone.

Definition. Let $\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k\}$ be a linearly independent set in an inner product space $V$. The Gram-Schmidt procedure produces orthonormal vectors $\{\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{e}_k\}$ by the recurrence

$$\mathbf{u}_j = \mathbf{v}_j - \sum_{i=1}^{j-1} \langle \mathbf{v}_j, \mathbf{e}_i \rangle\, \mathbf{e}_i, \qquad \mathbf{e}_j = \frac{\mathbf{u}_j}{\|\mathbf{u}_j\|}.$$

Each step strips from $\mathbf{v}_j$ every component already captured by the previously built basis directions, leaving a residual that is orthogonal to all of them.

Worked example. Take $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 1, 0)^T$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (1, 0, 1)^T$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Set $\mathbf{e}_1 = \mathbf{v}_1 / \|\mathbf{v}_1\| = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1,0)^T$. Then

$$\mathbf{u}_2 = \mathbf{v}_2 - \langle \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{e}_1 \rangle\,\mathbf{e}_1 = (1,0,1)^T - \tfrac{1}{2}(1,1,0)^T = \tfrac{1}{2}(1,-1,2)^T,$$

so $\mathbf{e}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1,-1,2)^T$. One may verify $\langle \mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2 \rangle = 0$ directly.

Key theorem (QR decomposition). Every matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with full column rank factors as $A = QR$, where $Q$ has orthonormal columns and $R$ is upper triangular with positive diagonal. Gram-Schmidt is the algorithm that builds $Q$ column by column; the inner products $\langle \mathbf{v}_j, \mathbf{e}_i \rangle$ populate exactly the entries of $R$.

from sympy import Matrix, GramSchmidt, latex

vecs = [Matrix([1,1,0]), Matrix([1,0,1])]
orthonormal = GramSchmidt(vecs, orthonormal=True)
for v in orthonormal:
    print(latex(v))

The QR decomposition cascades into virtually every domain where structured computation matters. In numerical linear algebra, it underlies the stable solution of least-squares problems $\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|$, replacing the numerically hazardous normal equations $A^T A \mathbf{x} = A^T \mathbf{b}$ with a triangular back-substitution. In signal processing, orthonormal bases allow a signal to be expanded in terms of independent frequency or wavelet components, each coefficient extractable by a single inner product with no cross-contamination. In machine learning, QR-based methods stabilize training of deep networks and underpin dimensionality reduction techniques such as PCA, where the leading eigenvectors of a covariance matrix are computed via orthogonal iterations built on repeated Gram-Schmidt sweeps.

Each application inherits the same gift: coordinates in an orthonormal frame are independent, unit-scaled, and geometrically interpretable—properties that collapse algebraic complexity and numerical error simultaneously.

The reader who has followed this book to its end now holds a complete toolkit: inner products to measure, projections to decompose, and Gram-Schmidt to build. The natural next step is to open one of these doors in earnest. Consider least-squares regression: take a real dataset, form the design matrix $A$, factor it as $QR$ using the procedure derived here, and solve the regression problem by back-substitution. Every formula reduces to the ideas on these pages, and the derivation will feel—correctly—like a payoff already earned.
