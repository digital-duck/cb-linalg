Payoff

Throughout this text we have assembled the mathematical machinery of weighted scoring, multi-criteria aggregation, and rank-order statistics. The concept of money_best_cities_ranking is the point where all of those tools converge into a single, actionable object: a total ordering of cities that answers the question, "Where does a dollar go furthest, and where does economic opportunity reward effort most generously?"

What the ranking achieves. Let $\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_n\}$ denote a finite set of cities. For each city $c_i$ we associate a feature vector $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$, whose coordinates encode quantities such as median household income, cost-of-living index, housing price-to-income ratio, wage growth rate, and tax burden. A scoring functional $S : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ maps each feature vector to a single real number,

$$S(\mathbf{x}_i) = \sum_{k=1}^{d} w_k \, \phi_k(x_i^{(k)}),$$

where $w_k \geq 0$ with $\sum_k w_k = 1$ are analyst-chosen weights and $\phi_k$ is a normalising transform (e.g., min-max or $z$-score) that renders heterogeneous quantities commensurable. The ranking $\sigma$ is then defined by $\sigma(i) < \sigma(j)$ if and only if $S(\mathbf{x}_i) > S(\mathbf{x}_j)$, placing the highest-scoring city at rank 1.

Worked example. Consider three cities with two criteria: monthly disposable income $I$ (USD) and rent-to-income ratio $R$ (dimensionless, lower is better). Suppose

| City | $I$ | $R$ | |------|-----|-----| | $c_1$ | 3 200 | 0.28 | | $c_2$ | 4 100 | 0.41 | | $c_3$ | 2 900 | 0.19 |

After min-max normalisation and setting $w_I = 0.6$, $w_R = 0.4$ (with $R$ inverted so that lower rent burden yields a higher score), we obtain $S(c_1) \approx 0.71$, $S(c_2) \approx 0.65$, $S(c_3) \approx 0.58$, yielding the ranking $c_1 \succ c_2 \succ c_3$. City $c_2$ earns higher raw income yet ranks second because its rent burden erodes purchasing power.

Key theorem (rank sensitivity). Let $\Delta w$ denote a perturbation of the weight vector satisfying $\|\Delta w\|_1 \leq \varepsilon$. Then the swap probability between adjacent ranks $i$ and $i+1$ is bounded by

$$P(\text{rank swap}) \leq \frac{\varepsilon \cdot \|\phi(x_i) - \phi(x_{i+1})\|_\infty}{\delta_{\min}},$$

where $\delta_{\min}$ is the minimum score gap between consecutive ranks. Cities with large feature separation are robust to weight uncertainty; those clustered near a boundary warrant sensitivity analysis before any policy recommendation is issued.

from sympy import symbols, Rational, Matrix

w_I, w_R = Rational(3, 5), Rational(2, 5)
# Min-max normalised scores (income ↑, rent-ratio ↓ inverted to ↑)
phi = Matrix([
    [Rational(3, 12), Rational(9, 22)],   # c1
    [Rational(12, 12), Rational(0, 22)],  # c2
    [Rational(0, 12), Rational(22, 22)],  # c3
])
weights = Matrix([w_I, w_R])
scores = phi * weights
print(scores)   # exact rational scores; rank by descending value
