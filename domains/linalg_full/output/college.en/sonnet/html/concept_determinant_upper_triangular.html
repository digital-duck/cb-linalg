Determinant Upper Triangular

Definition. A square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is called upper triangular if every entry below the main diagonal is zero, that is, $a_{ij} = 0$ whenever $i > j$. Equivalently,

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} & \cdots & a_{1n} \\ 0 & a_{22} & \cdots & a_{2n} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & a_{nn} \end{pmatrix}.$$

The central result governing such matrices is strikingly simple: the determinant of an upper triangular matrix equals the product of its diagonal entries.

Worked Example. Consider the $3 \times 3$ upper triangular matrix

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 & -1 \\ 0 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Expanding along the first column at each stage (or applying the theorem directly), the off-diagonal zeros eliminate all but the diagonal terms:

$$\det(A) = 2 \cdot \det\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix} = 2 \cdot \bigl(3 \cdot (-2) - 4 \cdot 0\bigr) = 2 \cdot (-6) = -12.$$

Equivalently, $\det(A) = a_{11} \cdot a_{22} \cdot a_{33} = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot (-2) = -12$.

Key Theorem. If $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is upper triangular with diagonal entries $a_{11}, a_{22}, \ldots, a_{nn}$, then

$$\det(A) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{ii}.$$

Proof sketch. Apply cofactor expansion along the first column. Because $a_{i1} = 0$ for $i \geq 2$, only the $(1,1)$-cofactor survives, yielding $\det(A) = a_{11} \det(A_{11})$, where $A_{11}$ is the $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ upper triangular submatrix obtained by deleting row 1 and column 1. This submatrix is itself upper triangular, so the same argument applies recursively. After $n$ steps, $\det(A) = a_{11} a_{22} \cdots a_{nn}$. $\blacksquare$

An immediate corollary is that $A$ is invertible if and only if none of the diagonal entries is zero.

Lab Cell (SymPy).

from sympy import Matrix, det, symbols

a, b, c = symbols('a b c', real=True)
A = Matrix([[2, 5, -1],
            [0, 3,  4],
            [0, 0, -2]])

print("det(A) =", det(A))          # -12

B = Matrix([[a, 1, 0],
            [0, b, 2],
            [0, 0, c]])

print("det(B) =", det(B))          # a*b*c

Running this cell confirms both the numerical result and the symbolic identity $\det(B) = abc$, illustrating that the theorem holds for arbitrary diagonal entries.
