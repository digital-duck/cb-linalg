Eigenvalue Eigenvector

Definition. Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over $\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{C}$). A nonzero vector $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is called an eigenvector of $A$ if there exists a scalar $\lambda$ such that

$$A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}.$$

The scalar $\lambda$ is called the eigenvalue associated with $\mathbf{v}$. Geometrically, multiplying by $A$ does not rotate $\mathbf{v}$; it only stretches or reflects it by the factor $\lambda$.

To find eigenvalues, rearrange the defining equation as $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$. For a nonzero solution $\mathbf{v}$ to exist, the matrix $A - \lambda I$ must be singular, which requires

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = 0.$$

This is the characteristic equation of $A$. Expanding the determinant yields a degree-$n$ polynomial in $\lambda$, called the characteristic polynomial. Each root of this polynomial is an eigenvalue of $A$.

Worked Example. Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. The characteristic polynomial is

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = \det\begin{pmatrix} 3-\lambda & 1 \\ 0 & 2-\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (3-\lambda)(2-\lambda) = 0,$$

giving eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$. For $\lambda_1 = 3$, solve $(A - 3I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$:

$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

For $\lambda_2 = 2$, solving $(A - 2I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ yields $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

Theorem (Spectral Theorem). If $A$ is a real symmetric matrix ($A = A^\top$), then all eigenvalues of $A$ are real, and eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. Consequently, $A$ admits the decomposition $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$, where $Q$ is orthogonal and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$.

Proof sketch. Suppose $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ is an eigenvalue with eigenvector $\mathbf{v}$. Then $\bar{\mathbf{v}}^\top A \mathbf{v} = \lambda \|\mathbf{v}\|^2$. Since $A$ is symmetric, the left side is also real, forcing $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. Orthogonality of eigenvectors follows from $(\lambda_i - \lambda_j)\langle \mathbf{v}_i, \mathbf{v}_j \rangle = 0$. $\square$

Lab Cell.

from sympy import Matrix, symbols, latex

A = Matrix([[3, 1], [0, 2]])
lam = symbols('lambda')

char_poly = A.charpoly(lam)
print("Characteristic polynomial:", latex(char_poly.as_expr()))

eigendata = A.eigenvects()
for val, mult, vecs in eigendata:
    print(f"lambda = {val}, multiplicity = {mult}, eigenvector = {vecs[0].T}")

Running this cell confirms $\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = 2$ and their associated eigenvectors, bridging the symbolic derivation to computational verification.
