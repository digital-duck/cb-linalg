Determinant Of Transpose

Definition. Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix. The transpose of $A$, denoted $A^{\top}$, is the matrix whose $(i,j)$-entry equals the $(j,i)$-entry of $A$. The determinant of the transpose theorem states:

$$\det(A^{\top}) = \det(A).$$

This result holds for every square matrix over any field, and it implies that any property of the determinant stated in terms of rows has an exact analogue in terms of columns.

Worked Example. Consider the $3 \times 3$ matrix

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & 5 \\ 1 & 0 & 6 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Expanding along the first row gives $\det(A) = 1(24-0) - 2(0-5) + 3(0-4) = 24 + 10 - 12 = 22$.

Its transpose is

$$A^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 0 \\ 3 & 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Expanding along the first row: $\det(A^{\top}) = 1(24-0) - 0 + 1(10-12) = 24 - 2 = 22$. Both values agree, confirming the theorem.

Key Theorem. For any $n \times n$ matrix $A$, $\det(A^{\top}) = \det(A)$.

Proof sketch. Recall the Leibniz formula:

$$\det(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i,\sigma(i)}.$$

For $A^{\top}$, the $(i,j)$-entry is $a_{j,i}$, so

$$\det(A^{\top}) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{\sigma(i),i}.$$

Substituting $\tau = \sigma^{-1}$ and using $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma^{-1}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)$ yields exactly $\det(A)$. $\blacksquare$

A direct corollary is that $A$ is singular if and only if $A^{\top}$ is singular, since $\det(A^{\top}) = 0 \iff \det(A) = 0$.

Lab Cell (SymPy).

from sympy import Matrix, det

A = Matrix([[1, 2, 3],
            [0, 4, 5],
            [1, 0, 6]])

print("det(A)  =", det(A))
print("det(Aᵀ) =", det(A.T))
print("Equal?  =", det(A) == det(A.T))

Running this cell prints det(A) = 22, det(Aᵀ) = 22, and Equal? = True, providing immediate computational verification. Students are encouraged to substitute random integer matrices and observe that equality persists universally.
